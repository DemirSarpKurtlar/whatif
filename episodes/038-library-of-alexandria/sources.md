# Sources / fact notes — 038 What if the Library of Alexandria had never burned?

## No single fire — a slow decline (historians agree on the shape, disagree on the details)
- Julius Caesar's fire during the siege of Alexandria (48 BC) damaged part of the library or warehouses near the harbour; how much was lost is unclear,
  and the library survived as an institution afterwards. Decline is usually dated from Ptolemy VIII's purge of intellectuals (145 BC), then loss of
  royal funding/membership under Roman rule, and damage in Aurelian's war (270s AD).
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Alexandria · https://www.history.com/news/library-of-alexandria-destroyed
- The Serapeum (the "daughter library") was destroyed in 391 AD under Bishop Theophilus. **Disputed:** whether it still held books by then
  (some ancient writers imply its shelves were already empty). Script says only "torn down", not "burned with its books".
- The 642 AD "Caliph Omar burned it" story is a later legend (first appears ~500 years later) — not used in the script.

## How many scrolls? (estimates vary widely)
- Modern estimates range roughly 40,000 to 400,000 scrolls (some versions/ancient claims go up to 500,000-700,000; ancient numbers are not reliable,
  and a scroll ≠ a "book" — one work could fill many scrolls). Script: "forty thousand ... or four hundred thousand. Nobody knows."
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Alexandria

## Works we know existed that are lost (lost to the world — not necessarily *because* of the library; most ancient texts vanished because nobody re-copied them)
- Aristotle's published "exoteric" dialogues: famous in antiquity (Cicero praised their "golden flow" of style); today only fragments in later authors.
  What survives is mostly school/lecture texts. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Works_of_Aristotle
- Eratosthenes' Geographika (3 books, with his world map): lost probably by the 2nd century AD; 155 fragments survive, mostly via Strabo
  (Roller, *Eratosthenes' Geography*, Princeton 2010). https://muse.jhu.edu/article/460747/pdf · https://www.strabo.ca/Eratosthenes.pdf
- Aristarchus of Samos (c. 310-230 BC): his heliocentric hypothesis is known only from Archimedes' *Sand Reckoner*; the work itself is lost.
  ~280 BC to Copernicus' *De revolutionibus* (1543) ≈ 1,800 years. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristarchus_of_Samos
- Hero of Alexandria (c. 10-70 AD; dating debated) described the aeolipile, a spinning steam turbine ("first recorded steam engine";
  Vitruvius mentions a similar device earlier). Note: Hero's *Pneumatica* partly SURVIVES — script does not call it lost, only says Alexandria
  "already had a spinning steam engine". It was a demonstration device, never used for work. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero_of_Alexandria
  · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeolipile

## The speculation (clearly a question, not a claim)
- "Could science have leapt a thousand years ahead?" is framed as an open question. Many historians argue the library's survival alone would not
  have triggered an industrial/scientific revolution (cheap slave labour, no demand for engines, economics and institutions mattered more; also
  many texts survived elsewhere via copies in Constantinople and the Islamic world). Others argue continuous scholarship could have sped things up.
  End card small print: "SPECULATIVE · ESTIMATES VARY".
