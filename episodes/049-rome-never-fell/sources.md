# Sources / fact notes — 049 What if the Roman Empire never fell?

(Wikipedia was not reachable from this session's network; facts were cross-checked through search results quoting the pages below.)

## The fall
- Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus, the last Western emperor, on 4 September 476; 476 is the conventional end of the Western Roman Empire
  (a convention: in practice it had been failing for decades). The Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire lasted until 1453.
  https://www.britannica.com/biography/Odoacer · https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/year/476 · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deposition_of_Romulus_Augustus
  Script: "In four seventy-six, the West collapsed."

## Population — "sixty million people, a fifth of humanity"
- Scholarly estimates for the 2nd-century peak: Walter Scheidel 59-72 million (c. 165 AD, just before the Antonine Plague); Kyle Harper ~75 million;
  a conservative ~65 million. With a world population of ~300 million, that is roughly 20% (one in five). No census exists for the whole empire, so all are estimates.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demography_of_the_Roman_Empire · https://unrv.com/empire/roman-population.php
  Script says "sixty million" (low end of the range) and "a fifth of humanity".

## Roads — "four hundred thousand kilometres"
- The Roman road network: more than 400,000 km of roads, of which over 80,500 km were stone-paved; 29 great military highways radiated from Rome;
  the late Empire's provinces were linked by 372 great roads. (400,000 km is usually given as a minimum estimate.)
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_roads · https://interestingengineering.com/culture/all-roads-really-did-lead-to-rome

## Aqueducts — "eleven aqueducts feeding one city of a million"
- The city of Rome was supplied by eleven aqueducts (the eleventh, Aqua Alexandrina, built 226 AD). Combined capacity estimates vary from about
  520,000 to over 1,100,000 m³ per day (so the script does not give a water figure). Frontinus, water commissioner c. 97 AD, wrote *De aquaeductu*.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_aqueducts_in_the_city_of_Rome · https://historycrunch.com/roman-aqueducts/
- City of Rome at its 2nd-century peak: roughly 1 million people (estimates up to ~1.5 million). https://www.worldatlas.com/ancient-world/how-big-was-the-city-of-rome-at-its-peak.html

## Concrete — "the Pantheon's dome still stands unreinforced, nineteen centuries later"
- The Pantheon (current building under Hadrian, c. 118-128 AD) has a 43.3 m dome; interior height to the oculus equals the diameter (a 43.3 m sphere fits inside);
  oculus 8.7 m. It is still the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. ~1,900 years old.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon,_Rome · https://www.enr.com/articles/39149-the-worlds-top-10-largest-domes
- Why Roman concrete lasts: MIT-led study (Seymour et al., Science Advances, Jan 2023, "Hot mixing") — lime clasts from hot-mixing with quicklime let cracks self-heal
  (cracked lab samples sealed in ~2 weeks). https://phys.org/news/2023-01-secrets-self-healing-roman-concrete.html  (Not stated in narration; background for visuals.)

## "A Greek gear computer that predicted eclipses"
- Antikythera mechanism: ancient Greek geared bronze device (30+ surviving gears; reconstructions ~37), found in 1901 in a shipwreck dated c. 70-60 BC off Antikythera;
  device dated variously c. 205-87 BC. Hand-cranked, it modelled Sun and Moon positions and predicted eclipses (Saros 223-month cycle).
  It is Greek, from the Roman Republic era, so the script calls it "a Greek gear computer".
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antikythera_mechanism

## "A spinning steam engine, sixteen centuries before a useful one"
- Hero of Alexandria (Roman Egypt; usually dated c. 10-70 AD) described the aeolipile, a spinning steam reaction turbine; Vitruvius mentions a similar
  device earlier (30-15 BC). It was a demonstration/curiosity, never used for work.
- First commercially successful (useful) steam engine: Thomas Newcomen, 1712 → about 1,650 years after Hero ("sixteen centuries").
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero_of_Alexandria · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_steam_engine

## "Instead, Rome shrank to maybe thirty thousand people"
- The city's population fell from about a million (2nd century) to roughly 30,000 by c. 550 AD (estimates 20,000-30,000 for the early medieval low).
  https://msaag.aag.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/26_Twine.pdf · https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii106/articles/marco-d-eramo-the-not-so-eternal-city
  Script hedges with "maybe".

## The speculation (clearly marked "maybe")
- "Maybe steam trains by the year one thousand. Or maybe it just crumbles slower." — pure speculation, framed with "maybe" twice and an on-screen
  "SPECULATION" label + "?" seal. Many historians argue Rome lacked the incentives for industrialisation (cheap slave labour, no coal-and-iron economy,
  no patent/market system), so survival alone would not guarantee an industrial revolution. End card small print: "SPECULATIVE · POPULATION FIGURES ARE ESTIMATES".
